# From Policy Gradient to GAE: Understanding Generalized Advantage Estimation

> **Paper:** *High-Dimensional Continuous Control Using Generalized Advantage Estimation*  
> **Authors:** John Schulman, Philipp Moritz, Sergey Levine, Michael I. Jordan, Pieter Abbeel  
> **Paper history:** arXiv preprint in 2015; published at ICLR 2016  
> **Paper link:** [arXiv:1506.02438](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02438)

## The short version

Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) is not a policy optimizer. It is an estimator for the advantage function, designed to make policy-gradient updates less noisy while retaining control over bias.

The key idea is simple:

> **One-step TD trusts the critic too much; a full Monte Carlo return trusts a random rollout too much. GAE uses $\lambda$ to interpolate between them.**

The paper combines this estimator with a trust-region policy update, particularly TRPO. Keeping those roles separate prevents a common conceptual mistake:

- **GAE** constructs an estimate of $A(s_t,a_t)$.
- **The value function** supplies the learned baseline and bootstrapping signal.
- **TRPO** controls how far the policy moves after the batch has been collected.

<Callout type='definition' title='One-sentence mental model'>
GAE is an exponentially weighted average of multi-step advantage estimators. Its $\lambda$ parameter controls how far we propagate temporal-difference evidence into the past.
</Callout>

## 1. Why policy gradients need an advantage estimate

For a stochastic policy $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$, the policy-gradient theorem can be written as

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,Q^{\pi}(s_t,a_t)
\right].
$$

The action-value $Q^{\pi}(s_t,a_t)$ is a valid learning signal, but it can have high variance. We can subtract a state-dependent baseline without changing the expected policy gradient:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{a_t\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)}
\left[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)b(s_t)\right]=0.
$$

Choosing $b(s_t)=V^{\pi}(s_t)$ gives the advantage function:

$$
A^{\pi}(s_t,a_t)=Q^{\pi}(s_t,a_t)-V^{\pi}(s_t).
$$

It measures whether the selected action was better or worse than the policy's average action at that state. In practice, the true $Q$ and $V$ are unknown, so both must be estimated from sampled trajectories.

## 2. The two endpoints: one-step TD and Monte Carlo

### 2.1 One-step TD residual

Given a learned value function $V_\phi$, define the one-step residual

$$
\delta_t^V=r_t+\gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1})-V_\phi(s_t).
$$

If $V_\phi$ were exact, the conditional expectation of this residual would equal the true advantage. In a real training run, the critic is imperfect, so this estimator is locally cheap but sensitive to value-function error.

At the first endpoint, $\lambda=0$:

$$
\hat A_t^{\mathrm{GAE}(\gamma,0)}=\delta_t^V.
$$

This usually gives lower variance, but it relies heavily on the critic.

### 2.2 Monte Carlo return

At the other endpoint, use a full discounted return:

$$
\hat A_t^{\mathrm{MC}}=\sum_{l=0}^{T-t-1}\gamma^l r_{t+l}-V_\phi(s_t).
$$

This reduces dependence on bootstrapping from $V_\phi$, but the estimator inherits every random fluctuation in the future trajectory. In long-horizon continuous-control tasks, its variance can make policy updates unstable.

The practical question is therefore not “TD or Monte Carlo?” but:

> **How much future evidence should be trusted before the estimator becomes too noisy?**

## 3. From $k$-step returns to GAE

The $k$-step advantage estimator can be expressed as a sum of residuals:

$$
\hat A_t^{(k)}
=
\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\gamma^l\delta_{t+l}^V.
$$

Different values of $k$ create a family of estimators. Small $k$ bootstraps quickly from the value function; large $k$ uses more trajectory evidence.

GAE avoids choosing one single $k$ by taking an exponentially weighted combination:

$$
\hat A_t^{\mathrm{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)}
=
\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(\gamma\lambda)^l\delta_{t+l}^V.
$$

The parameter $\lambda$ determines how quickly the influence of later residuals decays.

### 3.1 What $\lambda$ changes

When $\lambda=0$, only the current residual is used. As $\lambda$ approaches $1$, longer sequences are included and the estimate approaches a Monte Carlo-style return, assuming the rollout is sufficiently long and the boundary is handled correctly.

| Estimator | Dependence on $V_\phi$ | Variance | Typical role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One-step TD | High | Low | Fast local signal |
| GAE, small $\lambda$ | Moderate to high | Low to moderate | Stable actor–critic updates |
| GAE, large $\lambda$ | Lower | Higher | Longer credit assignment |
| Monte Carlo | Lowest bootstrapping dependence | Highest | Episodic reference |

This is the central bias–variance trade-off. A larger $\lambda$ is not automatically better; the useful setting depends on rollout length, reward scale, critic quality, and the size of each policy update.

### 3.2 $\gamma$ and $\lambda$ are different controls

- $\gamma$ changes how the task values future rewards.
- $\lambda$ changes how far TD residual information is propagated when constructing the estimator.

They appear together as $\gamma\lambda$ in the reverse recursion, but they answer different questions. Confusing them makes hyperparameter tuning unnecessarily opaque.

## 4. The $\gamma$-just perspective

The paper uses the notion of a $\gamma$-just estimator to clarify what “unbiased” means. The estimator is unbiased for the policy gradient of the $\gamma$-discounted objective, not necessarily for an undiscounted objective.

This distinction matters because discounting is already part of the objective. Even a perfect estimator can be “biased” relative to a different objective if the objective itself uses a different discount factor.

## 5. Reward shaping intuition

GAE can also be understood through potential-based reward shaping. The TD residual contains the shaped signal

$$
r_t+\gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1})-V_\phi(s_t).
$$

The critic provides a potential function over states. Summing residuals telescopes much of the potential difference, while the $\gamma\lambda$ weighting decides how far that shaped evidence is carried backward.

This interpretation explains why GAE is more than “just a smoother return”: it changes the temporal distribution of the learning signal without changing the basic policy-gradient structure.

## 6. How GAE fits into TRPO

The batch-level training loop can be summarized as follows:

```python title="gae_trpo_loop.py"
initialize policy parameters theta
initialize value parameters phi

for iteration in range(num_iterations):
    trajectories = collect_rollouts(policy=theta)

    # Use the old value function for this batch.
    deltas = rewards + gamma * next_values - values
    advantages = discounted_reverse_sum(deltas, discount=gamma * lam)

    theta = trpo_update(theta, trajectories, advantages)
    phi = trust_region_value_update(phi, trajectories)
```

The order is important:

1. Collect trajectories with the current policy.
2. Compute residuals and GAE using the pre-update value function $V_{\phi_i}$.
3. Use the fixed advantages for the TRPO policy update.
4. Update the value function to obtain $V_{\phi_{i+1}}$.

If the critic were updated first and overfit the same batch, its Bellman residuals could become artificially small. GAE would then shrink and erase useful policy-gradient information.

TRPO approximately solves a constrained policy-update problem:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{minimize}_{\theta}\quad &L_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\theta)\\
\text{subject to}\quad &D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{avg}}
(\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}\Vert\pi_\theta)\leq\epsilon.
\end{aligned}
$$

The division of labor is therefore:

- **GAE:** reduce or control the variance of the policy-gradient signal.
- **TRPO:** keep the policy update within a trust region.

## 7. Implementation: the reverse recursion

The infinite sum is never computed literally. For a finite rollout, scan backward:

```python title="gae.py"
def compute_gae(rewards, values, dones, last_value, gamma=0.99, lam=0.95):
    advantages = torch.zeros_like(rewards)
    gae = torch.zeros_like(last_value)

    for t in reversed(range(len(rewards))):
        next_value = last_value if t == len(rewards) - 1 else values[t + 1]
        nonterminal = 1.0 - dones[t]
        delta = rewards[t] + gamma * next_value * nonterminal - values[t]
        gae = delta + gamma * lam * nonterminal * gae
        advantages[t] = gae

    returns = advantages + values
    return advantages, returns
```

The mask is one of the most important details:

- **Termination:** the environment has reached a genuine terminal state; there is no future value to bootstrap from.
- **Truncation:** the rollout ended because of a time limit or batch boundary; depending on the task, bootstrapping may still be correct.

Treating both cases as the same `done` flag can systematically change the target.

After computing advantages, many implementations normalize them within the batch:

$$
\tilde A_t=\frac{\hat A_t-\operatorname{mean}(\hat A)}
{\operatorname{std}(\hat A)+\varepsilon}.
$$

Normalization can make optimization calmer, but it does not repair a wrong termination mask, a poor critic, or a mis-scaled reward.

## 8. Open-source code and reproduction resources

The paper was written in an ecosystem that has since changed substantially. It is useful to distinguish the historical experiment framework from later, easier-to-read implementations:

- [rllab](https://github.com/rll/rllab): a historical continuous-control and TRPO framework associated with the research ecosystem of the period. It is useful for reading older implementations, but it is no longer actively maintained.
- [OpenAI Baselines `ppo1/pposgd_simple.py`](https://github.com/openai/baselines/blob/master/baselines/ppo1/pposgd_simple.py): the `add_vtarg_and_adv` function shows reverse GAE accumulation, TD($\lambda$) value targets, and advantage normalization.
- [OpenAI Baselines `ppo2/runner.py`](https://github.com/openai/baselines/blob/master/baselines/ppo2/runner.py): a clear example of using `nextnonterminal` to distinguish true termination from a rollout boundary and to compute `mb_advs`.
- [OpenAI Spinning Up TRPO documentation](https://spinningup.openai.com/en/latest/algorithms/trpo.html): a useful overview of how TRPO and advantage estimation fit together.
- [Reproducibility_GAE](https://github.com/lweitkamp/Reproducibility_GAE): a teaching and reproduction repository for comparing Monte Carlo, TD, and GAE implementations.

These links should not all be described as the paper authors' official reproduction code. A more precise classification is: `rllab` is close to the historical experiment framework; Baselines and Spinning Up are later public implementations; and `Reproducibility_GAE` is primarily educational. Running old rllab code may require resolving Python, Theano, MuJoCo, and Gym compatibility issues.

## 10. Practical diagnostics

Before changing $\lambda$, log the distribution of:

1. value targets;
2. raw advantages;
3. normalized advantages;
4. value-prediction error; and
5. episode lengths, termination rates, and truncation rates.

If raw advantages are dominated by a few outliers, normalization may calm the optimizer without fixing the underlying credit-assignment problem. If value error grows while the policy loss looks healthy, the critic may be providing a misleading baseline.

## 11. Main takeaways

### Takeaway 1: GAE is not an isolated formula

The paper's contribution is a coherent combination of advantage estimation, value-function learning, and constrained policy optimization. GAE is the estimator inside that larger training design.

### Takeaway 2: The real tension is between two error sources

Small $\lambda$ means greater dependence on a possibly biased critic. Large $\lambda$ means greater exposure to rollout randomness. GAE makes that tension explicit and tunable.

### Takeaway 3: GAE and TRPO solve different problems

GAE answers “how good was this action?” TRPO answers “how far should the policy move?” Combining them does not make them interchangeable.

### Takeaway 4: The old critic is used deliberately

Advantages should be computed from the value function associated with the data-collection policy. Updating the critic first can make the estimator depend on a model that has already adapted to the same batch.

### Takeaway 5: Defaults are starting points, not rituals

Values such as $\gamma=0.99$ and $\lambda=0.95$ are common starting points. They should be checked against rollout length, reward scale, critic quality, and the actual estimator statistics.

## 12. Self-test questions

1. Why can subtracting $V(s_t)$ reduce policy-gradient variance without changing its expectation?
2. What does the one-step residual estimate when the value function is exact?
3. Why does $\lambda=0$ depend more heavily on the critic than $\lambda\approx1$?
4. Why is a truncated rollout not necessarily the same as a terminal transition?
5. Why should the critic be frozen while computing advantages for a policy update?
6. Why is GAE an exponentially weighted average of all $k$-step estimators?
7. What does $\gamma$ change, and what does $\lambda$ change?
8. Which part of the pipeline is responsible for estimating the signal, and which part controls the policy step size?

## Conclusion

GAE is best remembered as a controlled interpolation between two imperfect sources of evidence. One-step TD is efficient but trusts the critic; Monte Carlo is direct but noisy. By exponentially weighting future TD residuals, GAE makes the bias–variance trade-off explicit through $\lambda$.

The paper then combines this estimator with TRPO, turning a more reliable advantage signal into a conservative policy update. In implementation, the most consequential details are often not the headline equation but the reverse scan, the bootstrap value, the termination/truncation mask, the order of critic and policy updates, and the diagnostic plots around them.

> **GAE is an exponentially weighted average of multi-horizon advantage estimators. It uses $\lambda$ to balance trust in the critic against trust in the sampled rollout, while TRPO controls how that signal changes the policy.**
